## The framework in one paragraph

Subsurface sewage disposal in Tennessee is governed by Chapter 0400-48-01 of the state's rules, administered by TDEC's Division of Water Resources and delivered through county environmental health offices. It covers system design, tank capacity and construction, disposal field design, installer licensing, and permits for pumping contractors. Davidson County is one of the contract counties, which can mean an additional county permit alongside the state process.

## Tank capacity and construction

Tanks are sized against the dwelling's design flow, which follows bedroom count. The Tennessee Private Sewage Disposal Code sets minimum liquid capacity in its sizing table, with 750 gallons as the practical floor for the smallest dwellings; 1,000 to 1,500 gallons covers the great majority of single-family homes in Davidson County.

Rule 0400-48-01-.09 governs tank design and construction, including a requirement that cast-in-place concrete tanks over 1,000 gallons have minimum top and bottom thicknesses of six inches. Rule 0400-48-01-.08 covers capacity; Rule 0400-48-01-.07 covers the conventional disposal field.

The practical relevance for a homeowner is the bedroom count. Adding a bedroom to a house served by a system permitted for fewer is both an engineering problem and a permit problem.

## Who is allowed to do what

**Installation and repair:** a licensed subsurface sewage disposal system installer. Not a general contractor, not a plumber without the endorsement, and not the homeowner. TDEC or the local inspector signs off before backfill.

**Pumping and hauling:** a Septic Tank Pumping Contractor permit, issued by TDEC, is required for removing and disposing of domestic septage from septic tanks, holding tanks, portable toilets and similar facilities. Applications go on form CN-0765 to the Division of Fiscal Services.

**Vehicle marking:** every vehicle engaged in domestic septage removal must carry the firm or operator name and address plus the removal permit number in bold lettering at least two inches high on a contrasting background, and display a department-issued identifying sticker.

That last requirement is the homeowner's verification tool. It is visible from the driveway.

## Permits, and how long they take

Any new system, or a repair to a faulty existing one, needs a septic system construction permit from the county environmental health office before work starts. The application covers site details, occupancy, bedroom count, water use, basement plumbing, and the installer's name, plus a site sketch showing property lines, house, well, spring, driveway and utilities.

Review generally takes about 10 days and must be completed within 45 days of submission. Soil maps from a soil scientist are required where the site calls for them, and larger conventional or alternative systems need an engineered design.

## What the rules do not say

There is no blanket statutory requirement that a Tennessee homeowner pump a conventional residential septic tank on a fixed schedule. The three-to-five-year interval is engineering practice, and it is the interval that keeps solids out of the drain field — but it is guidance, not law.

There is also no blanket state requirement for a septic inspection at the point of sale. In practice buyers, agents and lenders request one often enough that it functions as a condition of most transactions, and Tennessee's residential property disclosure obligations still apply to defects the seller knows about.

Permitted alternative and engineered systems are different: those can carry operating, monitoring and maintenance conditions written into the permit itself, and those conditions are enforceable.
