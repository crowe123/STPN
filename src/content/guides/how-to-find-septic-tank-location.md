## Step 1: the permit file

Every septic system installed in Tennessee needed a construction permit from the county environmental health office, and the application required a sketch showing property lines, house site, well and spring locations, driveway and utilities.

For anything installed in the last few decades that file is the fastest route to the answer, and it is a phone call rather than an afternoon with a probe. It also tells you the tank capacity and the bedroom count the system was permitted for, both of which are worth knowing independently.

## Step 2: which way does the drain leave?

Inside the house, find where the main building drain exits. The best clue is the concentration of plumbing — the wall with the most fixtures behind it, or where the main stack drops. An accessible cleanout on an exterior wall marks the line directly.

Septic tanks sit on that alignment, typically **10 to 20 feet from the wall**, and downhill wherever the grade allowed. That single observation narrows a half-acre lot to a strip.

## Step 3: read the ground

Tanks leave surface signatures, easiest to see in dry weather or early morning dew:

- **Differential grass growth** — a rectangle that browns first in drought, because there is less soil above it holding moisture
- **Slight settlement** — a shallow depression over the lid where backfill consolidated
- **A change in grade or a firm patch** underfoot compared to surrounding lawn
- **Snow melting differently** over the tank, if you happen to be looking in the right season

Walk the likely alignment slowly rather than scanning the whole yard.

## Step 4: probe, do not dig

A soil probe pushed into the likely area finds the tank top with pinhole-sized disturbance. Work systematically along the alignment established in step 2, spacing probes about a foot apart, feeling for the sudden hard stop of concrete at a consistent depth.

Digging on speculation is what produces a ruined lawn and a tank that is still not found. Excavation should be the last few minutes of the process, confined to uncovering the access port itself once the probe has located it.

## Where tanks sit on Davidson County lots specifically

**Post-war and mid-century housing — Madison, Donelson, parts of Antioch.** Modest lots, tank usually in the side or back yard within easy reach of the house, lid at or just below grade at installation and now under six decades of soil and turf.

**Steep and wooded lots — Bellevue ridges, Joelton, Goodlettsville, Forest Hills.** Gravity dictated everything. The tank is downhill, often much further from the house than owners expect — sixty to eighty feet is not unusual.

**Landscaped properties — Green Hills, Oak Hill.** The tank is where it always was; what changed is the mature planting, terracing and hardscape now sitting over it. This is where record-reading earns its keep, because the cost of probing in the wrong place is an established bed rather than a patch of lawn.

**Older lots with additions.** Check whether an extension was built over the original drain alignment. It happens, and it moves the search.

## When you find it, make it permanent

Mark the position and record it measured from two fixed points — house corners work well. That survives new owners, new landscaping and forgotten memories.

Better, fit a riser. It brings the access port to ground level with a sealed, secured lid, so every subsequent service starts at the lid instead of at a probe. It also makes the small maintenance jobs practical — rinsing an effluent filter, checking sludge depth — that otherwise never happen because getting to the tank is a project.

The lid must be bolted or twist-locked. An at-grade access port with a loose slab over it is genuinely dangerous around children and animals.
