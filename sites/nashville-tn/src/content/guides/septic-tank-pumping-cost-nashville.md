## The short answer, with the sources

For a standard 1,000-gallon residential tank in Davidson County, budget **$300 to $525**.

That sits inside the statewide picture. Septic pumping across Tennessee runs **$275 to $525**. Davidson County homeowners specifically have reported a wider band of **$199 to $796**, and the national average is **$428**, with most households paying between **$292 and $564**.

Those are published figures, not our invention, and the spread between them is the interesting part.

## What actually moves your number

Three things, in order of how much they matter.

### Access — the biggest single factor

If your tank lids are at grade, whether because risers were fitted or because the tank sits shallow, pumping is quick and you pay the bottom of the range. If the lids are buried under six or eight inches of soil, turf, landscaping or a later-added terrace, someone has to find them and hand-dig to them first. National cost data puts that surcharge at **$50 to $200**.

In several parts of Davidson County this is the norm rather than the exception. Joelton's wooded ridge lots accumulate decades of leaf litter over the access points. Forest Hills and Oak Hill have half-acre-plus lots with eighty years of mature landscaping over them. On those properties, access is most of the difference between one quote and another.

### Tank capacity

A 1,500-gallon tank holds half again what a 1,000-gallon tank does, and costs proportionally more to empty and dispose of. Mobile home systems frequently run 500 to 750 gallons and cost less per visit — though they need pumping more often, so the annual cost of ownership evens out.

### How overdue it is

A tank pumped on schedule is a straightforward job. A tank that has gone ten years or more may have sludge compacted at the floor and a hardened scum mat that a pump hose cannot lift, which turns a pump-out into a **tank cleaning** at $400 to $650. That is not an upsell; it is a genuinely longer job.

## The number nobody quotes you against

Here is the figure that makes the whole conversation make sense.

> Drain field replacement in Tennessee runs **$7,000 to $15,000 and up**. Nashville-area drain field repair has been reported from **$2,000 to above $10,000**.

And in Middle Tennessee those numbers sit at the higher end, because our soils drain slowly. Nashville is in the Central Basin, where limestone dissolves into fractured bedrock and the soil above is often shallow clay loam. Slow percolation means a field needs more absorption area for the same daily flow, so fields here are built large — and replacing a large field costs more than replacing a small one.

Set against that, a $400 pump-out every three to five years is not really an expense. It is the thing that stops the five-figure one.

## What a proper job should include for that price

At the range above, you should be getting all of this:

- Both access points opened, not just the nearest
- Full removal of liquid, floating scum **and** settled sludge, then a backflush
- Sludge and scum depths measured and recorded before pumping
- Inlet and outlet baffles inspected while the tank is empty
- The effluent filter located, cleaned and inspected — or a clear statement that there isn't one
- A written record of what was found and a recommended interval based on your tank

Most pumpers check baffles as part of standard service at **$0 to $50**, so there is no good reason for it to be missing.
