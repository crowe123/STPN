## Two different risks

When people ask about a sinkhole near their septic system, they are usually picturing the dramatic one. The quieter risk is the more common problem.

### 1. Physical undermining

Karst forms because limestone dissolves. Voids develop below the surface, and when the roof of one fails you get a sinkhole. If that happens beneath or beside a drain field, the field can be undermined — laterals displaced, aggregate lost into the void, or a section of field collapsed outright.

This is the obvious risk, and it is real, but it is not the one that catches most people.

### 2. Effluent reaching groundwater untreated

This is the important one, and it is invisible from the surface.

A drain field is not a hole for liquid to disappear into. It is a treatment device. Effluent percolates slowly through soil, and that soil does the biological filtration that makes the discharge safe before it reaches groundwater.

Fractured limestone short-circuits that entirely. Where rock is close to the surface and fractured, effluent can enter the fractures and travel without ever passing through an adequate soil column. **From the surface the system looks like it is working perfectly** — nothing ponds, nothing smells, no drains back up — while it is failing at the only job that actually matters.

That is why Tennessee regulates subsurface sewage disposal at all, and why county environmental health offices across Middle Tennessee watch karst features closely and enforce generous setbacks from any visible solution feature.

## Signs your lot is karst-influenced

- Visible sinkholes, closed depressions or swallow holes
- Rock outcrops at or near the surface
- Very shallow soil over rock when anything is dug
- Surface water disappearing at a point rather than running off
- Neighbouring properties with known karst features

Davidson County sits squarely in this landscape, and the county's own history reflects it — the 1946 decision to require half-acre minimum lots, and larger where soils were poor, was a response to exactly this shallow-limestone base.

## What a proper assessment looks at

- Where solution features sit relative to the field, and whether setbacks are met
- Soil depth over bedrock across the field footprint, not just at one probe
- Whether effluent is being treated or simply disappearing
- Whether a well is on the parcel, and the separation and relative elevation between it and the field
- Whether reserve area exists for a replacement, given the karst constraints

That last point matters more on karst lots than anywhere, because the parcel may be large while the *usable* area for a system is small.

## If you also have a well

The stakes rise considerably. When one parcel both draws drinking water and disposes of waste underground, the separation between them is the protection, and Tennessee's rules set minimum distances accordingly. On fractured limestone, effluent can travel further and faster than the same distance through intact soil would suggest.

Surfacing effluent on a property with a well should be treated as urgent. Periodic well water testing is sensible for any private well, and particularly after any septic failure or repair.
