> This is a general walkthrough of how a typical job runs, written to set expectations for people who have never had a septic tank serviced. It is illustrative rather than an account of a specific customer's property.

## Before the truck arrives

The call establishes three things: the address, the approximate tank size if the owner happens to know it, and what the system is currently doing. Not knowing the tank size is completely normal and changes nothing — it is measured on site.

What does change the visit is access. Whether the lid position is known, whether there is a riser, how far the truck can get, whether there is a gate. On a Bellevue or Joelton property with a long drive and a downhill tank, that conversation decides which truck is scheduled.

## Locating and opening

On a property with a riser, this stage takes a minute: lift the secured lid and start.

Without one, the sequence is records, then the building drain's exit point, then a surface read for settlement or differential grass growth, then probing along the likely alignment. Excavation is the last step and is confined to uncovering the access port. Anything that involves digging gets quoted before it happens.

## The readings that come first

Before the vacuum starts, three measurements go on the sheet:

- **Liquid level relative to the outlet invert.** Above it means effluent is not leaving — the field, not the tank, is the constraint.
- **Scum depth.** How much floating material has built up and whether it is nearing the outlet.
- **Sludge depth.** What is on the floor, which against tank capacity and the date of the last service gives the accumulation rate.

These only exist before pumping. A visit that skips them has thrown away the diagnostic value of opening the tank at all.

## Pumping

Liquid and scum come off first. Then the sludge, worked with the hose until the tank floor is visible.

On a tank serviced within five years that is straightforward. On one at ten or twelve years the floor layer has dewatered and compacted, and a hose alone passes over it — that needs agitation and backflushing with recovered liquid, then a second vacuum pass. It is a different job and the price reflects it. Either way, the standard is a visible floor, not a lower level.

## Inspection, with the tank empty

This is the part that produces information. With nothing obscuring the interior:

- Inlet baffle or tee — present, intact, correctly set
- Outlet baffle — the one that matters most, and the one most often gone on pre-1990s concrete tanks
- Effluent filter, if fitted — located, rinsed, condition noted
- Tank walls, floor and lid — cracks, root intrusion, movement
- Riser and lid seal, where fitted

Photographs go with it, because a written finding backed by an image is what an insurer or a buyer's agent accepts.

## The brief, before leaving

What was found, in plain terms. What it means for this property. What needs doing now versus what is worth monitoring. And a date for the next service, calculated from the sludge accumulation rate rather than quoted from a rule of thumb.

That date is the deliverable most people find genuinely useful, because it converts an open-ended worry into a diary entry.

## What a rushed visit leaves out

For comparison, the shortcuts that a homeowner cannot see from the driveway: no pre-pump readings, liquid drawn off with the sludge left on the floor, the baffles never looked at, the effluent filter never located, and no written record. The invoice looks the same. The tank is back at capacity far sooner, and nothing was learned.
