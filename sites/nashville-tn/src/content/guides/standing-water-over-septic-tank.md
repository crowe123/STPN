## Do these now

1. **Keep people and pets off it.** Surfacing effluent is a biohazard, whatever caused it.
2. **Reduce water use immediately.** Spread laundry out, take shorter showers, fix any running toilet. Every gallon is going to a system that has nowhere to put it.
3. **Do not mow it** or drive anything over it. Saturated ground compacts, and compaction makes a field worse.
4. **Call for a pump-out and a diagnosis** — not just an empty-and-go.

## Is it actually the septic?

Worth checking, because heavy rain confuses this.

**It is the system if:** water stands over the tank or field while the rest of the yard has drained; there is odour; the grass over that area is noticeably greener or growing faster; or it persists in dry weather.

**It may be weather if:** the whole yard is saturated equally, it clears at the same rate as everywhere else, and there is no odour or differential grass growth.

If it happens **only** after heavy rain and clears within a few days, you may be looking at seasonal saturation rather than system failure — which is a real distinction, covered below.

## What causes it

**A genuinely full tank.** Overdue pumping, sludge at outlet height. The straightforward case, fixed by pumping.

**A clogged effluent filter or failed outlet baffle.** Both restrict flow and back the system up. Cheap to resolve — $0 to $600 between them — and both are found by opening the tank.

**A drain field that has stopped accepting.** Years of solids reaching the field have sealed the soil interface. Pumping relieves it temporarily; the field is the problem.

**An unlevel distribution box.** Flow going overwhelmingly to one lateral, which drowns while the rest of the field sits unused. Common on sloped ground — Bellevue's ridge lots especially — inexpensive to correct, and frequently missed.

**Seasonal groundwater saturation.** In the Whites Creek bottoms and out in Bells Bend, the water table rises and the soil around the laterals is already full. The field has no absorption capacity left regardless of its condition. A system that struggles after wet weather and behaves in dry spells is describing exactly this.

**Surface water running onto the field.** Roof runoff, a driveway shedding across it, regrading that redirected drainage. The field is absorbing rainfall on top of household flow.

## The Middle Tennessee dimension

Surfacing effluent here is not only a yard problem.

Nashville sits on karst — limestone dissolved into fractured bedrock, frequently under shallow soil. Fractured rock lets poorly treated effluent reach groundwater without the filtration a proper soil column provides, which is the reason Tennessee regulates these systems and why county environmental health offices enforce setbacks from visible solution features.

If you also have a **well** on the property, treat surfacing effluent as urgent without qualification, and consider water testing afterwards.

## What a proper response looks like

Pump the tank down to take load off the field. Check the effluent filter and both baffles while it is empty — the cheap causes, ruled out first. Open the distribution box and confirm it is level and clear. Walk the field to establish whether surfacing is localised or across the whole area. Then assess whether this is loading, mechanical, or soil-driven.

Only after that is a repair-or-replace conversation worth having. A company that quotes a $12,000 field without opening the distribution box has skipped the cheap answers.
