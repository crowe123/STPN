## Two requirements to satisfy, twice

Subdividing a lot with a septic system means both resulting parcels have to work. For the parcel keeping the existing residence, Tennessee requires:

1. The septic system **completely contained within its own property**, with specific setbacks observed.
2. **Sufficient area within the parcel to replace** the existing system if it fails.

And for any new parcel intended to carry a residence, you need somewhere a system can actually be permitted — which means a site evaluation including a soil morphology assessment by a soil scientist, adequate percolation, and setbacks.

Fail either and you have created a problem that surfaces later, expensively.

## How this goes wrong

The failure mode is consistent. Someone splits off the back of a lot, or adjusts a line with a neighbour, and the land that goes is the land the drain field would have been replaced onto.

The house keeps working. The system keeps working. Nothing is visibly wrong for years — until the field fails, or until a sale triggers an inspection, and the discovery is that there is nowhere to put a replacement.

Metro's own notice on this does not soften the consequences:

> On occasion, failure to comply with these requirements leads to significant problems for current or future owners. On very rare occasions, a residence may be condemned.

## Make the call before the survey

Metro anticipates exactly this situation and offers help with it:

> Although it is the owner's responsibility to comply with these requirements, the Metro Public Health Department will assist you in determining an acceptable location of the revised property boundaries.

**Environmental Engineering Services: 615-340-5604.**

That is the single most useful thing in this guide. A conversation before the surveyor draws the line costs nothing. Redrawing a boundary afterwards involves lawyers, neighbours and, sometimes, buying land back at a price set by someone who knows you need it.

## Why the usable area is smaller than you think

Reserve area has to be **suitable**, not merely vacant. It needs adequate soil depth over bedrock, acceptable percolation, and the required setbacks from wells, boundaries and structures.

Middle Tennessee's geology tightens all of that. Shallow clay loam over fractured limestone means slow percolation, which means larger fields, which means more reserve area needed. On karst lots, setbacks from visible solution features remove more. A two-acre parcel can have surprisingly little genuinely usable ground.

## Before you commit

- Locate the existing system and confirm it sits within the proposed retained parcel with setbacks
- Identify where a replacement field could go, and confirm the soil there supports one
- Check the new parcel can be permitted at all before pricing it as a building lot
- Talk to Metro before the survey
- Get it in writing

## Bottom line

The land you sell off may be the land the house needs in fifteen years. One call to **615-340-5604** before the survey prevents a problem that is very hard to undo. For an assessment of where your system sits and what your parcel retains, call **(615) 234-9048**.
