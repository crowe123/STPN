## The short answer

**Negotiable.** Nothing in Tennessee law assigns the cost. It falls where your contract puts it.

The common default is that the **buyer pays**, because the inspection is a due-diligence exercise conducted for their benefit, by a company they chose, producing a report they can rely on.

## What it costs

| | Cost |
|---|---|
| Real estate septic inspection, including the pump-out | $350–$600 |
| Standalone inspection without pumping | $225–$400 |
| Routine pump-out on its own | $300–$525 |

Inspection and pumping are quoted together for a straightforward reason: a full tank cannot be inspected. Baffles, tank structure and the effluent filter are simply not visible until the tank is empty.

## Why sellers increasingly pay for their own

The buyer-pays default has an awkward property: it hands the buyer a report, a deadline, and every incentive to translate findings into money.

Consider a corroded outlet baffle.

**Found by the seller before listing:** a $150 to $600 repair, booked at leisure, and the system is sound when the listing goes up.

**Found by the buyer's inspector during the contingency period:** a negotiation, in which "the septic needs work" tends to become a credit worth several times the actual repair, because the buyer is pricing uncertainty rather than the part.

The inspection costs the same either way. What differs is who is holding the information and how much time they have.

## The two Davidson County records worth pulling either way

**The permitted bedroom count.** Metro Public Health holds the approval date, inspection dates and the bedroom count the residence is approved for. This matters because Tennessee makes it illegal to advertise a home for more bedrooms than its septic is approved for. A mismatch found before listing is a problem to solve; found during the inspection period, it is a problem with a deadline attached.

**The reserve area.** Tennessee requires the system to sit within its own parcel with setbacks, and requires the parcel to retain area to replace the system if it fails. Metro notes that non-compliance causes significant problems for current or future owners, and that a residence may in rare cases be condemned. Worth knowing before a buyer's inspector raises it.

## Who pays for what happens next

Separate question, and also negotiable. Common outcomes:

- Seller repairs before closing
- Seller credits the buyer at closing
- Price adjusted to reflect the work
- Buyer accepts the condition as disclosed

Which one you land on depends far more on how much time is left on the clock than on what the repair actually costs. That is the real argument for inspecting early.

## Bottom line

Buyers usually pay; sellers increasingly should. Either way, get the pumping quoted with the inspection and get the bedroom-approval record pulled. Call **(615) 234-9048** with your closing date.
