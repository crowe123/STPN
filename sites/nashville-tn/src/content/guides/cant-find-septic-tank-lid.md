## This is normal

Tanks disappear. Lids get covered by regrading, turf, decades of leaf litter, later landscaping, a deck, or a driveway extension nobody thought about. Records are lost between owners. On older Davidson County properties the tank was installed by someone long gone and never marked.

If you have bought a house and nobody can tell you where the tank is, you are in ordinary company.

## Start with the records

The step most people skip, and it frequently saves an hour of probing.

**Metro Public Health** holds septic records for existing residences in Davidson County — approval date, inspection dates, and the bedroom count the system was approved for. That tells us the system's era and likely configuration before anyone touches a shovel.

**Environmental Engineering Services: 615-340-5604.** You will need the property's map and parcel number, which Metro's online property information will give you.

## Then work from the house outward

The tank is not randomly placed. Wastewater leaves the house through a single outlet line, and that line runs to the tank — usually in a fairly straight run, generally within ten to twenty-five feet of the building.

So the method is:

1. **Find where the plumbing exits.** Usually identifiable from the basement or crawlspace, or from the position of the main stack.
2. **Follow that line outward.** A probe pushed gently into the ground at intervals along the projected run will strike the tank.
3. **Map the edges.** Once you hit it, probing around establishes the tank's outline.
4. **Locate both access points.** Residential tanks have two — one over the inlet baffle, one over the outlet. Finding only the nearest is how outlet baffles go uninspected for decades.
5. **Hand-dig to the lids.** Not machine-dig. An excavator near a concrete lid risks cracking it.

## What not to do

**Do not dig exploratory holes across the lawn.** Probing finds the tank without wrecking the yard.

**Do not use a machine.** The cost of a cracked lid or tank shoulder dwarfs any time saved.

**Do not assume the neighbour's layout matches yours.** Even on similar-era houses, placement varies with the plumbing exit and the ground.

**Do not drive a probe with force.** Firm and steady finds concrete. Hard and fast can go through a lid.

## Where this is most common in Davidson County

Certain areas produce these calls disproportionately, and for identifiable reasons.

**Joelton and Whites Creek** — wooded ridge and bottomland lots where decades of leaf litter and regrading have put access points under six or eight inches of soil.

**Forest Hills and Oak Hill** — half-acre-plus lots with eighty years of mature landscaping over them, where terraces and plantings have gone in above tanks that predate them.

**Bells Bend farms** — where outbuildings and older structures sometimes tie into systems nobody documented, and finding a second tank that has never been pumped is not unusual.

## The permanent fix

Once the lids are exposed, fit **risers**. At $300 to $600 they bring both access points to ground level for good.

The arithmetic is straightforward: you will pump every three to five years for the remaining life of the system, and hand-digging a buried lid adds $50 to $200 each time. Risers pay for themselves across two or three visits, and they make an emergency call dramatically faster — which is worth more than the money at the moment you need it.

Fitted during a pump-out you were having anyway, you pay for one excavation instead of two.
