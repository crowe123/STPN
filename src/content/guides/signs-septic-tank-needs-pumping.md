## Stop-now signals

**Sewage backing up into the house.** Through a floor drain, a shower tray, or the lowest toilet on the ground floor. Stop using water completely — no flushing, no laundry, no dishwasher, no showers. Every gallon added makes the situation worse and increases what ends up inside the building. Raw sewage carries bacteria, viruses and parasites; keep people and pets out of the affected area.

**Effluent standing on the ground over the drain field.** Dark, wet, foul-smelling ground in the field footprint means liquid is surfacing because it has nowhere else to go. It is a contamination issue as well as a system failure, particularly on a property with a private well.

Either of these is an emergency call rather than a booking.

## Days-not-months signals

**Every drain in the house slow at the same time.** A single slow fixture is a local blockage. Slow drains in the kitchen, the bathroom and the utility room simultaneously is a system-level problem — the tank cannot accept what the house is sending it.

**Sewage odour indoors.** Gas escaping into the building means the system is under enough pressure to push past traps and vents. It is also a hydrogen sulphide exposure worth taking seriously in an enclosed space.

**A pump or aerobic unit alarm sounding.** That alarm exists specifically to give warning before a backup. It is not a nuisance light.

## Due-soon signals

**Gurgling toilets, or water rising in a toilet when the washing machine drains.** The system is displacing air because it cannot take the volume quickly enough. This is the earliest reliable signal most households notice.

**Spongy or unusually green grass over the field.** Effluent reaching the topsoil layer rather than dispersing at depth. In Middle Tennessee's clay soils this shows up first after wet spells, which is worth noting: a field that is soft only in spring may be at its capacity rather than failed.

**More than five years since the last documented pump-out.** Statistically due regardless of symptoms. If there is no record at all, treat it as overdue.

## The one that catches people out

A blocked effluent filter produces every symptom of a full tank in a tank that is not full. Many Davidson County systems installed in the last two decades have one fitted in the outlet baffle, and it needs rinsing at every service.

It is a cheap component protecting an expensive one, and it is the most common reason a system that was pumped last year is misbehaving again this year. Ask whether the filter was located and cleaned; if the answer is that there was not one, ask how that was confirmed.

## What the symptoms do not tell you

None of these signals distinguishes between a full tank and a failed drain field, and the difference is between a $275 to $525 pump-out and a $6,000 to $10,000 replacement.

The distinguishing measurement is the liquid level in the tank relative to the outlet invert, read before anything is pumped. A level standing above the outlet means effluent is not leaving — the field is the problem, not the tank. Pumping in that situation buys days, because the tank simply refills from the house.

That is why the reading is taken first. It is also why an inspection carried out after someone else has already pumped the tank cannot answer the question.

## What to do, by signal

| What you are seeing | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Sewage indoors or surfacing outside | Stop using water. Call for emergency dispatch. |
| All drains slow, odour indoors, alarm sounding | Reduce water use. Book within days. |
| Gurgling, soft ground, over 5 years since service | Book a pump-out with inspection on your own timeline. |
| Nothing, but no service record | Book a baseline pump-out and get the readings taken. |
