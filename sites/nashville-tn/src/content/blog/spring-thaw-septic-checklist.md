Spring is the most informative time of year for a septic system in Middle Tennessee, and the reason is unflattering: wet ground exposes problems that dry ground hides.

A drain field with reduced absorption capacity can keep up perfectly well through a dry August. Put it under weeks of saturated soil and a rising water table, and the margin disappears. What surfaces in March and April is a fair picture of how much capacity your field has actually got left.

That makes spring worth using rather than waiting out.

## Walk the field after a wet spell

Give it a day or two after heavy rain, so the rest of the yard has drained, then walk it.

**Compare it to the surrounding lawn.** Ground that stays soft or soggy over the field while everywhere else has dried is the field, not the weather. That distinction is the whole test.

**Look for differential growth.** Grass over the field growing noticeably faster or greener than the rest means effluent is reaching the surface and fertilising it.

**Use your nose.** Odour outdoors near the tank or field, particularly after rain, means gases or effluent are escaping where they should not.

**Note where.** Wet in one area rather than across the whole field often points at an unlevel distribution box sending flow to one lateral. That is inexpensive to correct, and much better found now than in July.

## The bottomland problem

If your property sits in the Whites Creek bottoms, out in Bells Bend, or anywhere else low-lying, spring may be showing you something different: seasonal groundwater saturation.

When the water table rises, the soil around your laterals is already full. The field has no absorption capacity available regardless of its condition, and no amount of pumping changes that. The signature is a system that struggles after wet weather and behaves impeccably in a dry month.

It is worth naming accurately, because the response is different. Diverting surface water away from the field and levelling the distribution box does more here than a pumping schedule ever will.

## Check what winter did to access

Frozen and thawed ground moves. Lids that were findable in October can be sitting under settled soil by March, and if you have had any ground disturbance over the winter it is worth confirming you can still locate both access points.

If you cannot, that is the argument for risers. At $300 to $600 fitted once they remove the problem permanently, and they make an emergency call in the middle of a wet spell dramatically faster.

## Reassess your interval before summer

Spring is a sensible time to book if you are due, for a practical reason: you would rather have the tank emptied before the summer months when household water use tends to rise — more laundry, more guests, more irrigation running near the field.

If you do not know when it was last pumped, that is itself the answer. Tennessee guidance is every three to five years, and every two to three with a garbage disposal, a larger household or a smaller tank.

## What to keep off the field

Now that the ground is soft, this matters more than usual:

- No vehicles, trailers or heavy equipment over the field — saturated soil compacts, and compaction reduces absorption permanently
- No new plantings of anything with aggressive roots
- Keep downspouts and driveway runoff directed away from the field
- Do not build the spring project — shed, patio, pool — over the field or its reserve area

That last one matters more than most people realise. Tennessee requires the parcel to retain sufficient area to replace the system if it fails, and building over that reserve creates a problem that surfaces at sale.

## If something looks wrong

Reduce water use, keep people and pets off any wet area, and get it looked at while it is still showing you the symptom. A field assessed in spring while it is visibly struggling is far easier to diagnose than the same field in September, when it will look fine and tell you nothing.
