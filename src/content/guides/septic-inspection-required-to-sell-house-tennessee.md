## What the law actually requires

Tennessee has no general statute compelling a septic inspection before a residential sale. What it does have is a residential property disclosure regime: sellers are obliged to disclose known defects, and a septic problem the seller is aware of falls squarely inside that.

The practical position that follows is uncomfortable for anyone hoping to avoid the question. You are not required to inspect. You are required not to conceal what you know. And the moment a buyer's inspector finds something, the question of what the seller knew becomes live.

## What buyers and lenders ask for anyway

Across Davidson County transactions involving a septic system, the common requests are:

- A recent pump-out receipt
- A written condition report covering tank, baffles and effluent filter
- Confirmation that the permit record matches what is on the ground
- Sometimes, a drain field assessment where there is any surface evidence

Some loan products and some buyers' agents treat these as conditions. Others do not raise them until an inspection flags something. Either way, the seller's exposure is the same.

## The asymmetry that makes pre-listing inspection worth it

A defect found by the seller's inspector is a repair with a quote attached, addressed on the seller's timeline, with the option to shop the work.

The same defect found by the buyer's inspector three days before closing is a negotiation in which the seller has no time, no alternative buyer, and a contract with a deadline. Sellers routinely concede several multiples of the actual repair cost in that position, because the alternative is the deal collapsing and the listing returning to market carrying a disclosed problem.

That asymmetry is the entire argument, and it does not depend on the system being in bad shape. A clean report is worth having too — it removes a contingency from the transaction.

## What the report should contain

An inspection worth showing a buyer's agent covers:

- Pre-pump liquid, scum and sludge readings, which only exist before pumping
- A full pump-out so the interior is genuinely visible
- Inlet and outlet baffle condition, photographed
- Effluent filter presence, condition and service
- Tank wall, floor, lid and riser condition
- Surface assessment of the drain field footprint
- A dated, written conclusion with photographs

The pre-pump readings matter more than they sound. Liquid level standing above the outlet invert means effluent is not leaving the tank, which points at the field rather than the tank. An inspection carried out after someone else has already pumped has lost that reading permanently.

## Timing it

Four to eight weeks before listing. Recent enough that it will still be inside the six-month window most agents want at closing, early enough that anything found can be quoted, scheduled and completed without pushing the launch date.

While you are at it, pull the permit file from the county environmental health office. It typically records tank capacity, field size and the bedroom count the system was approved for — and it usually contains a site sketch, which is also the fastest way to locate an access lid nobody has seen in twenty years.

## What buyers should take from this

The mirror image applies. Buying a septic property with no documentation means inheriting an unknown, and the standard advice is a pump-out and inspection within 90 days of closing to establish a baseline. Readings taken shortly after closing describe the previous owner's usage, which is genuinely informative; wait two years and that information is gone.
