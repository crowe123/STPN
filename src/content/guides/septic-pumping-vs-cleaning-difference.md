## What is inside the tank

A septic tank holds three layers. Scum floats on top — fats, oils, soaps and anything else lighter than water. Clear effluent sits in the middle, and that middle layer is the only one supposed to leave, through the outlet baffle to the drain field. Sludge settles on the floor: the heavier solids that have dropped out of suspension.

Both the scum and the sludge are supposed to stay. Removing them is the whole point of the service.

## Pumping

Pumping draws off what the vacuum can lift. On a tank serviced at a sensible interval that is everything: three to five years of sludge is still wet and mobile, so the hose takes it, and the operator ends with a visible tank floor.

This is the routine service and it is what most Davidson County properties need most of the time. Published Tennessee pricing puts it at $275 to $525 per visit.

## Cleaning

Cleaning is what a tank needs when the sludge on the floor has stopped behaving like a liquid.

Left long enough, the bottom layer dewaters under its own weight and compacts into a dense, semi-solid mass. A vacuum hose passes over it. The truck fills with liquid, the operator sees the level drop, and the invoice says the tank was pumped — but the working volume never actually recovered.

The remedy is mechanical: bulk pump-out first, then agitation and backflushing with recovered liquid under pressure to break the compacted layer into a pumpable slurry, then a second vacuum pass. It takes longer, hauls more material, and costs accordingly.

## Which one a tank needs

**Pumping** — serviced within the last five years, a documented history, no symptoms, ordinary household usage.

**Cleaning** — ten or more years since service, or no record at all; a previous pumper reported the tank as pumped but symptoms returned within months; effluent leaving the tank looks grey and solid-laden; the outlet baffle is sitting in sludge rather than clear liquid; a property just taken over with unknown history.

The decision is made on site from the sludge depth measured through the access port before anything is pumped. It is not something to guess at from the driveway.

## Why the distinction costs money if it is wrong

The failure mechanism is the clear zone. As sludge builds from below and scum builds from above, the clear middle band narrows. The outlet baffle draws from that band, and when it narrows far enough the outlet starts drawing solids-laden liquid instead.

Those solids go to the drain field, where they blind the soil interface. That is the mechanism behind most drain field replacements, and the field is the part of the system that cannot be serviced. Middle Tennessee replacement quotes commonly run $6,000 to $10,000.

A tank that was pumped but not cleaned continues that process at close to full speed, while the owner believes the problem was dealt with. That is the practical cost of the distinction.

## The effluent filter, while we are here

Many Davidson County tanks installed in the last two decades carry an effluent filter in the outlet baffle. It needs rinsing at every service, pumping or cleaning.

A blocked filter produces every symptom of a full tank in a tank that is not full — slow drains, gurgling, backup. It is the single most common reason a system serviced last year is misbehaving again this year. Ask whether it was located and cleaned. If the answer is that the tank does not have one, ask how that was confirmed.
