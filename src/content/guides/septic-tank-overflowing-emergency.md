## What overflow actually means

Effluent appearing at the surface means the system has run out of anywhere to put water. It reaches the surface either through the tank access port and lid seal, or — more commonly — through the ground over the drain field, where the soil beneath the laterals can no longer accept flow and the liquid takes the only remaining route.

The mechanism is the same one that produces an indoor backup. Which direction it goes depends on the relative levels of the field, the tank and the building drain. A house on higher ground than its field overflows outside; a house level with or below it backs up inside.

## What to do right now

1. **Stop all water use in the building.** No flushing, laundry, dishwasher, showers or taps.
2. **Keep people and pets off the wet ground.** Mark it if children use the yard.
3. **Do not attempt to drain, dig into, or cover the area.** Digging into a saturated field compacts it and makes recovery less likely.
4. **On a well property, keep the surfacing area away from the well head** and stop drinking untreated well water until the system is cleared and the well tested.
5. **Call for priority dispatch** and describe where the effluent is surfacing.

## What the pump truck can and cannot fix

Pumping the tank down removes the immediate pressure and stops the overflow progressing. That is real relief and it is the right first action.

What it does not do is restore a drain field's ability to accept effluent. If the field has failed, the tank refills from normal household use within days and the overflow returns. The distinction is diagnosed at the tank:

- **Liquid level below the outlet invert:** effluent is leaving the tank normally. The overflow was a tank-capacity problem, and pumping resolves it.
- **Liquid level standing at or above the outlet invert:** effluent is not leaving. The field, not the tank, is the constraint.

That reading only exists before the tank is pumped, which is why it is taken first on every emergency call here.

## Wet weather, and why the timing matters

Middle Tennessee's soil is thin and clay-heavy over fractured limestone. Clay accepts water slowly at the best of times, and after sustained rain the soil column beneath a drain field saturates completely.

A field at its design limit copes through a dry summer and surfaces during a wet March. The pattern is diagnostic: a system that only overflows in wet weather is usually describing a capacity constraint rather than a dead field, and the cheaper interventions — diverting downspouts and driveway runoff away from the field footprint, reducing household flow, keeping vehicles off the laterals — are worth exhausting before a replacement quote.

The reverse pattern, surfacing in dry weather, is a much stronger indication that the soil interface has sealed and the field has genuinely reached the end of its life.

## The number the diagnosis is protecting

Drain field replacement in Middle Tennessee is commonly quoted at $6,000 to $10,000, against published 2026 national figures of roughly $5,000 to $20,000. Replacement also requires a construction permit from the county environmental health office under Tennessee's subsurface sewage disposal regulations, and on karst ground — which is most of Davidson County — the review enforces generous setbacks from any visible solution feature. That can mean the replacement field cannot go where the existing one is.

None of which is an argument for delay when a field has genuinely failed. It is an argument for establishing that it has, in writing, before the excavator arrives.
