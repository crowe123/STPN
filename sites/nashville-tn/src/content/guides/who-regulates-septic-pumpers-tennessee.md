## The regulation

Septic tank pumping contractors in Tennessee operate under **Tenn. Comp. R. & Regs. 0400-48-01-.20**, part of the rules governing subsurface sewage disposal systems. The broader framework sits with **TDEC's Subsurface Sewage Disposal Program**, which handles permitting for systems themselves and maintains lists of **licensed septic system installers and septic tank pumpers**.

Davidson County adds a layer. As a **contract county** it administers its own septic program, with Metro Public Health's Environmental Engineering Services (615-340-5604) handling soils interpretation, system design, percolation-test monitoring and groundwater protection locally.

## Why any of this is regulated

Because of what is in the tank and where it can end up.

Septage is not ordinary waste. Disposing of it improperly is a public health and groundwater problem, and in Middle Tennessee that risk is sharper than most places. This is karst country — limestone dissolved into fractured bedrock under frequently shallow soil — and fractured rock lets poorly treated material reach groundwater without the filtration an intact soil column provides.

That is the reason there are rules about who may pump a tank and where the contents may go, and it is the reason those rules are enforced with more attention here than the topic might suggest.

## What to check before hiring

Four questions, none of them awkward to ask.

**1. Are you licensed to pump septic tanks in Tennessee?**
TDEC publishes the lists. A legitimate operator answers immediately.

**2. Where does the septage go?**
There is a lawful answer and it has a cost attached. A company that is evasive here is worth avoiding, and a quote dramatically below the market may be explained by this.

**3. Will you pump the tank completely?**
Not a licensing question, but the most useful one. Full removal of liquid, scum and settled sludge, then a backflush — not a liquid draw-down that leaves the sludge behind.

**4. Will you inspect both baffles and the effluent filter?**
Most pumpers include this at $0 to $50. It is where problems actually get found.

## What licensing does not tell you

Licensing establishes that a company may lawfully do the work and dispose of the contents. It says nothing about whether they will open both lids, measure sludge depth, look at the outlet baffle, or tell you what they found.

Those are the differences that determine whether your drain field survives, and none of them appear on a licence. That is why the [pre-hire checklist](/resources/) asks about the work rather than the paperwork.

## For commercial and grease customers

Grease work carries an additional documentation requirement. Metro Water Services' FOG program requires **certified hauler manifests** and **three-year record retention**, alongside servicing traps at least every 30 days and interceptors at least every 90.

If you run a kitchen, the manifest is not administrative overhead. It is the evidence that the service happened, and it is what an inspector asks for.

## Bottom line

Pumpers are regulated under Tenn. Comp. R. & Regs. 0400-48-01-.20, TDEC publishes the licensed lists, and Davidson County runs its own program on top.
