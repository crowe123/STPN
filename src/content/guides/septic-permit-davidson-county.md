## What needs a permit and what does not

**No permit needed by the homeowner:** routine pumping, tank cleaning, inspection, effluent filter service, riser installation on an existing access port, and general maintenance that does not alter the system's design or disturb the disposal field.

**Construction permit required:** any new subsurface sewage disposal system, and any repair or replacement of a faulty one. That includes replacing a drain field, relocating a tank, and expanding a system to serve additional bedrooms. A licensed subsurface sewage disposal system installer must carry out tank placement and drainfield work, and the inspector signs off before backfill.

The permit sits with the county environmental health office, under the state's Regulations to Govern Subsurface Sewage Disposal Systems.

## The contractor's permit, which matters to you

Anyone removing and disposing of domestic septage in Tennessee must hold a **Septic Tank Pumping Contractor permit** issued by TDEC's Division of Water Resources, applied for on form CN-0765.

There is a visible check available to any homeowner: permitted vehicles must carry the firm or operator's name and address plus the domestic septage removal permit number, in lettering at least two inches high, in bold print on a contrasting background, and must display a department-issued identifying sticker.

If a truck pulls onto a property without that marking, the operator is not permitted to be hauling septage. It is the simplest verification available and it takes five seconds.

## What the application involves

The submission covers landowner name and address, the lot's location and size, number of occupants, number of bedrooms, water use amounts, whether there is an excavated basement, whether basement plumbing fixtures are installed, whether house and lot are staked, and the installer's name.

It must include a sketch showing property lines, house site, well location, spring location, planned driveway and utilities, plus directions to the site. Soil maps prepared by a soil scientist are required where the site warrants them, and larger conventional or alternative systems need a design from a licensed engineer.

Review generally takes about 10 days and must be completed within 45 days of submission.

## Why Davidson County reviews are stricter than you might expect

Middle Tennessee is karst country: soluble limestone bedrock with a thin soil mantle and solution features that route water underground rapidly and with very little filtration.

Environmental health offices in Davidson, Williamson, Maury and Rutherford counties watch karst features carefully and enforce generous setbacks from any visible solution feature. The reasoning is direct — a sinkhole can swallow a drainfield, and fractured limestone lets inadequately treated effluent reach groundwater without passing through the soil column that provides the treatment.

Practical consequences for a homeowner planning work:

- Adequate soil depth to bedrock has to be demonstrated, and on ridge lots it often is not
- A visible depression anywhere near the proposed field can move it, or rule the location out
- Alternative system designs are more common here than the state average, and they cost more
- A replacement field frequently cannot go where the existing one is

## The bedroom-count trap

Systems are permitted against a design flow tied to bedroom count. Finishing a basement, converting a bonus room, or adding an extension can push a house past what its system was approved to serve.

That becomes a problem twice: practically, because the system is now undersized and will fail early, and administratively, because it surfaces during a sale when a buyer's agent compares the permit file to the listing. It is far easier to resolve during planning than under contract.

Pulling the permit file is worth doing for any property with a septic system, and it is usually straightforward — the file typically includes a site sketch showing house, tank and field, which is also the fastest way to find a lost access lid.
