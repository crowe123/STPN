## The numbers

| Work | Cost |
|---|---|
| Field rejuvenation / minor repair | $1,000–$5,000 |
| Drain field repair, Nashville area | $2,000–$10,000+ |
| **Drain field replacement, Tennessee** | **$7,000–$15,000+** |
| Full system replacement (tank + field) | $15,000–$30,000 |

For comparison, the routine pumping that prevents most field failures runs $300 to $525.

## Why Middle Tennessee is at the expensive end

This is the part that gets left out of national cost guides.

Nashville sits in the Central Basin, where limestone dissolves into sinkholes, caves and fractured bedrock. The soils above are frequently shallow — clay loam over rock — and they drain slowly.

Drain field sizing is driven by percolation rate. Slow-percolating soil needs **more absorption area** to handle the same daily flow, so fields here are designed at the large end of the range. A larger field means more excavation, more aggregate, more distribution pipe, and more surface restoration afterwards.

That is why a replacement quote in Davidson County frequently lands above what a national average would suggest, and why the quote is not padded when it does.

## What drives your specific number

**Field size required.** Set by the permitted bedroom count and by your soil's percolation rate. A four-bedroom-approved system on slow soil needs a substantially larger field than a two-bedroom system on good soil.

**Soil and rock.** Shallow soil over limestone can mean rock removal, which is slow and expensive. Areas the county flagged historically for poor soils — Oak Hill was named specifically in the 1946 lot-size regulations — tend to be more difficult.

**Access for machinery.** A field reachable by excavator across open lawn is straightforward. One behind a house, up a slope, or past mature trees on a Forest Hills-type lot is not.

**System type.** A conventional gravity field is the baseline. Where soil or water table rules that out, the answer may be a mound system, a pressure distribution system or subsurface drip disposal — all of which cost more, and all of which Tennessee permitting recognises.

**Permitting and design.** Tennessee requires a site evaluation including a soil morphology assessment by a soil scientist. Davidson County administers its own program as a TDEC contract county.

**Reserve area.** Your parcel must retain enough room to site the replacement, with required setbacks. Where it does not, the problem becomes considerably harder — and this is worth checking before you need to.

## The cheaper things to rule out first

Before anyone quotes you for a field, these should be eliminated, because they produce identical symptoms at a fraction of the cost:

- **A blocked effluent filter** — $0–$280
- **A failed outlet baffle** — $150–$600
- **An unlevel or blocked distribution box**, which drowns one lateral while the rest of the field sits unused
- **Surface water running onto the field**, which is a drainage problem rather than a field failure
- **Seasonal groundwater saturation**, common in the Whites Creek bottoms and Bells Bend, where the field competes with a high water table

Any company that quotes a replacement without ruling these out first is quoting the most expensive possible answer to a question nobody has asked properly.

## When replacement is genuinely the answer

When the soil interface has been sealed by biomat across the field after years of solids reaching it, and removing the upstream cause plus resting the field does not restore function. At that point further repair spending is deferral, and an honest assessor will say so.
