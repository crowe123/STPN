## The mechanism

A drain field does not hold effluent, it hands it to the soil. That handover depends entirely on the soil column beneath the trenches having somewhere to put the water.

After sustained rain, it does not. The pore spaces are already full. A saturated soil column cannot accept effluent regardless of how sound the field is, so flow backs up into the tank, and once the tank reaches the building drain invert it backs up into the house.

Middle Tennessee makes this worse than average in two ways. The soil is clay-heavy, so it accepts water slowly at the best of times, and it is thin over fractured limestone, so there is less of it to work with.

## Where it hits hardest in Davidson County

**River and creek bottomland.** Pennington Bend and the ground toward the Stones River confluence in Donelson. Neelys Bend in Madison. Bells Bend. The Harpeth flats around River Plantation and McCrory Lane in Bellevue. Water tables here rise with the river, and the field can be sitting in saturated ground for weeks.

**Lots receiving concentrated surface water.** Downspouts discharging near the field, driveway runoff funnelled across the footprint, a neighbouring property draining onto the lot. This one is common everywhere and is the most fixable.

**Fields already at their design limit.** A system serving more people than it was permitted for copes in dry conditions and has no margin left when conditions turn.

## What the pattern tells you

This is the useful part. A system that only misbehaves in wet weather is describing a **capacity constraint**, not necessarily a dead field.

A field whose soil interface has genuinely sealed fails in dry weather too. One that only surfaces after a wet fortnight is more likely to be at its limit, and the cheaper interventions are worth exhausting before anyone quotes a replacement at $6,000 to $10,000:

- Divert downspouts and driveway runoff away from the field footprint
- Get vehicles, trailers and sheds off it
- Reduce household flow during wet spells — spread laundry out, fix running fixtures
- Reroute a water softener discharge if it goes into the tank
- Confirm the tank is not simply full, which is the other cause of the same symptom

## What to do when it happens

Stop using water. Call. And ask specifically for the liquid level to be read against the outlet invert **before** the tank is pumped — that reading is what separates a full tank from a field that cannot discharge, and it is destroyed by pumping.

Getting that in writing during a wet-weather event is also what makes the argument later, if a replacement gets proposed on the strength of one bad March.

## The timing that avoids it

Pumping in late summer or early autumn, when the ground is at its driest, has two advantages. Access is better, and the tank starts the wet season with full working volume rather than partial.

For a system with any history of seasonal trouble, that scheduling choice is free and it buys real margin.
