## What Tennessee actually requires

Three separate things get conflated here, so let us take them apart.

**Disclosure is required.** Sellers in Tennessee must disclose known septic system details before closing. If you know the field surfaces after heavy rain, that is a known detail.

**The inspection option must be presented.** Under **TREC Rule 1260-2-.37**, the buyer's agent is required to present the option of a sewer inspection. The buyer decides whether to take it up; the option has to be offered.

**Pumping is not universally mandated by statute.** There is no blanket state requirement to pump before every sale. Local requirements can differ, so checking with the local health department is worthwhile.

Where those three meet is the practical reality: **a septic system cannot be meaningfully inspected without emptying the tank.** You cannot assess baffles, tank structure or the effluent filter through a full tank. So although pumping is not legally mandated, an inspection that is worth anything involves it.

## Why sellers increasingly go first

The traditional pattern is that the buyer inspects during their contingency period. That puts you in the worst possible negotiating position: a buyer holding a fresh report, a deadline, and every incentive to convert a $600 finding into a $6,000 credit.

Inspecting before listing costs the same money and changes the dynamic entirely. You find out at your own pace, you fix what is cheap to fix, and you price the rest deliberately rather than reactively.

## The Davidson County records nobody checks

Two records exist that most transactions never look at, and both can derail a closing.

### The permitted bedroom count

Metro Public Health holds the approval date, inspection dates and **the number of bedrooms an existing residence is approved for**. This is not trivia. In Tennessee it is **illegal to advertise a home for more bedrooms than its septic is approved for**, and whoever advertises it is liable for misrepresentation.

A four-bedroom house on a three-bedroom-approved system is a genuine problem, and it is far better found before the listing goes up than during the inspection period.

### The reserve area

Tennessee requires a septic system to be **completely contained within its own parcel** with specific setbacks, **and** requires the parcel to retain **sufficient area to replace the system** if it fails. Metro is explicit that failure to comply causes significant problems for current or future owners, and that on very rare occasions a residence may be condemned.

Properties where a previous boundary change compromised the reserve area exist, and the problem tends to surface exactly when someone is trying to sell.

## What a transaction-grade inspection includes

- Full pump-out, so the tank can actually be seen
- Tank structure, both baffles and the effluent filter assessed and photographed
- Drain field walked and assessed for surfacing and capacity
- The permitted bedroom count retrieved and compared against the listing
- Reserve-area assessment
- A written report a buyer, agent and lender can all use

## Timing

Inspection periods are short. Book against your date, and confirm the report will land inside the contingency window — a report that arrives after it expires is worth nothing to the transaction.
