## The framework

Subsurface sewage disposal in Tennessee sits under Chapter 0400-48-01 of the state's rules, administered by TDEC's Division of Water Resources and delivered locally through county environmental health offices. Davidson County is one of the contract counties — alongside Blount, Hamilton, Jefferson, Knox, Madison, Sevier, Shelby and Williamson — which can mean an additional county permit alongside the state process.

Nothing in that structure is new in 2026. What is worth restating is which parts of it a homeowner actually touches.

## Four things that reach a homeowner

**A construction permit is needed for any new system, repair or replacement.** Not for pumping, cleaning, inspection or fitting a riser to an existing access port. Review generally takes about 10 days and must be completed within 45 days of application.

**Installation must be done by a licensed installer.** Tank placement and drainfield work require a licensed subsurface sewage disposal system installer, and the inspector signs off before backfill. There is no homeowner-install route.

**Pumping contractors must hold a TDEC permit.** A Septic Tank Pumping Contractor permit, applied for on form CN-0765, is required for removing and disposing of domestic septage.

**Permitted trucks must be marked.** Vehicles engaged in domestic septage removal must display the firm or operator name and address plus the removal permit number, in lettering at least two inches high, in bold print on a contrasting background, along with a department-issued sticker.

That last one is the most practically useful rule in the whole chapter for a homeowner, because it is a verification anyone can perform from their own driveway in five seconds.

## Tank sizing, and why it matters at renovation

Tanks are sized against design flow, which follows bedroom count, with 750 gallons the practical floor and 1,000 to 1,500 gallons covering most single-family homes. Rule 0400-48-01-.09 covers tank design and construction, including a six-inch minimum top and bottom thickness for cast-in-place concrete tanks over 1,000 gallons.

The renovation implication is the one people miss. Finishing a basement or adding a bedroom can push a house past what its system was permitted to serve. That is a permit matter as well as an engineering one, and it surfaces at the worst possible moment — during a sale, when a buyer's agent compares the permit file to the listing.

## Karst, and why Middle Tennessee reviews are stricter

Environmental health offices in Davidson, Williamson, Rutherford and Maury counties watch karst features carefully and enforce generous setbacks from any visible solution feature. Sinkholes can swallow a drainfield, and fractured limestone lets inadequately treated effluent reach groundwater without the filtering a proper soil column provides.

For anyone planning ahead: this can mean a replacement field is not permitted where the existing one sits. On a constrained lot that is worth knowing years before the current field fails.

## What the rules do not require

There is no blanket statutory pumping schedule for a conventional residential system in Tennessee. The three-to-five-year interval is engineering practice, not law.

There is also no blanket requirement for a septic inspection at sale, though buyers, agents and lenders request one often enough that it operates as a condition of most transactions — and residential property disclosure obligations still apply to defects a seller knows about.

Permitted alternative and engineered systems are the exception: those can carry enforceable operating and maintenance conditions written into the permit itself.
