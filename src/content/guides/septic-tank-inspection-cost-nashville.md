## Why it is priced with a pump-out

The defects worth paying to find are inside a full tank, hidden under liquid. A missing outlet baffle, a cracked wall, root intrusion at the inlet penetration, a floor that has heaved — none of them are visible with the tank in service.

So a genuine inspection is a pump-out plus the work of examining and documenting what the empty tank shows. Pricing it separately would be pricing a fiction. Expect the combined visit to sit at or above the published Tennessee pumping band of $275 to $525, moving with tank capacity, lid access and how long it has been since the last service.

## The readings that only exist before pumping

Three measurements are taken before the vacuum starts, and they cannot be recovered afterwards:

**Liquid level relative to the outlet invert.** The single most diagnostically useful number in the visit. Standing above the outlet means effluent is not leaving the tank, and the drain field — not the tank — is the constraint. Well below the outlet means the tank is leaking.

**Scum depth.** How much floating material has accumulated, and whether it is approaching the outlet baffle.

**Sludge depth.** How much settled solid is on the floor, which against tank capacity and the date of the last service gives an accumulation rate — and an accumulation rate gives a next-service date rather than a range.

An inspection commissioned after another contractor has already pumped the tank has lost all three. That is the main practical reason to book inspection and pumping as one visit.

## What gets found, in order of frequency

**Missing or deteriorated outlet baffles.** The most common significant finding, particularly on concrete tanks from the 1980s and earlier where the baffle was cast into the structure. The gas space above the liquid converts hydrogen sulphide to sulphuric acid on damp concrete, and decades of that attack concentrates exactly where the baffle sits. It leaves a stub, no above-ground symptom, and years of floating scum feeding the drain field. Very common through Donelson, Madison and Hermitage.

**Blocked or missing effluent filters.** A blocked filter produces every symptom of a full tank in a tank that is not full.

**Root intrusion at the inlet penetration.** Frequent on the wooded lots through Forest Hills and Bellevue.

**Cracked or displaced lids** letting surface water into the tank, which displaces working volume and pushes hydraulic load onto the field.

**Settled or heaved tank sections** on ground that has moved — a live concern on Davidson County's karst.

## What separates a report from a look

A useful report is dated, photographed and written so an insurer, an agent or a buyer can read it without translation. It states the three pre-pump readings, the condition of each component, and a plain conclusion: sound, monitor, or repair needed.

That document does three jobs beyond the visit itself. It is what an insurer assesses a later claim against. It is what a buyer's agent accepts at closing. And it is the baseline the next inspection is read against — four inches of sludge accumulation in three years is a different system from twelve, and only a documented prior reading makes that visible.

## When it is worth paying for

- Before listing a property, four to eight weeks out
- Within 90 days of buying a septic property
- Where symptoms returned soon after a recent pump-out
- Where there is no documented service history at all
- Where an insurer or lender has asked for system condition
- Where a replacement has been quoted and you want the failure confirmed before committing $6,000 to $10,000
