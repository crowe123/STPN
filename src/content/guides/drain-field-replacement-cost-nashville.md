## What the number covers

A drain field replacement is an excavation job, not a plumbing job. The commonly quoted Middle Tennessee range of **$6,000 to $10,000** typically covers site evaluation and soil work, the permit application, excavation of new trenches, aggregate and distribution piping, a new distribution box, connection to the existing tank, backfill and reinstatement.

Published 2026 national figures span roughly **$5,000 to $20,000**, and the top of that range describes sites where the field has to be relocated, an alternative system design is required, or engineered fill is needed because the soil column is inadequate.

## What moves it on a Davidson County property

**Field size.** Sized from the design flow, which follows bedroom count. A five-bedroom house needs materially more trench than a two-bedroom one.

**Soil depth to bedrock.** This is the Middle Tennessee constraint. Conventional trench fields need an adequate soil column above bedrock, and on the ridge ground through Bellevue, Joelton, Pasquo and Goodlettsville that depth is often marginal. Where it is inadequate, the answer is an alternative system design signed off by an engineer, and the price moves accordingly.

**Karst features.** Environmental health offices across Davidson, Williamson, Rutherford and Maury counties watch karst carefully and enforce generous setbacks from any visible solution feature — a sinkhole, a swallet, a depression. A lot with a feature near the existing field may have nowhere compliant to put a new one within the same footprint.

**Access.** An excavator has to reach the site. Long drives, mature trees, steep grades and narrow village lots — Old Hickory in particular — all add cost or force a smaller machine.

**Whether the tank goes too.** A field failure caused by a tank with a missing outlet baffle is not solved by a new field alone. If the tank is at the end of its life the replacement is a system replacement.

## Rule out the cheaper findings first

Three situations look identical from the yard and cost very differently to resolve.

**Temporary saturation.** After sustained rain the soil around a field saturates and it cannot discharge. Common through Pennington Bend, Neelys Bend and the Harpeth bottomland. The field is at its limit, not dead, and diverting surface water off the footprint is sometimes the entire fix.

**Hydraulic overload.** The field is structurally sound but receiving more water than it was sized for — after an addition, a larger household, a leaking fixture, or a water softener discharging into the tank. Fixing the input can bring it back.

**Genuine failure.** The biomat has sealed the trench walls and effluent cannot leave regardless of conditions. This one needs replacing.

The tank readings separate them. Liquid level standing above the outlet invert in dry weather, with solids evident at the outlet, is a strong failure indication. The same reading only after heavy rain points at saturation. A field that has never been rested, on a house that recently gained occupants, points at overload.

## What makes the money last

Once a new field is in, three habits determine whether it sees ten years or forty:

- Pump the tank on interval so solids never reach it
- Keep vehicles, trailers, sheds and pools off the footprint — compaction crushes laterals and seals soil
- Route downspouts and driveway runoff away from it

On clay soil that holds water rather than shedding it, the third one matters more here than it does in most of the country.
