## The decision in one sentence

A field that is failing **because of something else** can often be recovered. A field that has been sealed by years of solids is finished.

Everything below is about telling those two apart before spending money.

## Rule out the cheap causes first

This is the step that gets skipped, and skipping it is how people buy $12,000 fields they did not need.

**A clogged effluent filter.** Costs $0 to $280. Produces symptoms that look exactly like field failure — slow drains, backup, effluent struggling to leave the tank.

**A failed outlet baffle.** $150 to $600. When it fails, the scum layer leaves the tank directly. Fix the baffle and the field stops receiving what was damaging it.

**An unlevel or blocked distribution box.** This one is common and consistently missed. The distribution box splits flow between laterals. If it is not level, one lateral takes almost everything and drowns while the rest of the field sits barely used. The symptom is a field failing at a fraction of its capacity. Correcting the box can bring the unused portion back into service.

This is particularly common on sloped ground — Bellevue's ridge lots, for instance, where gravity already favours the downhill run before any settlement makes it worse.

**Surface water reaching the field.** Roof runoff, a driveway shedding across it, regrading that redirected drainage. The field is absorbing rainfall on top of household flow. That is a drainage problem, and diverting the water is inexpensive.

**Seasonal groundwater.** In the Whites Creek bottoms, Bells Bend and other low-lying ground, a high water table periodically saturates the soil around the laterals. The field then has no capacity for effluent regardless of its condition. A field that struggles after wet weather and behaves in dry spells is describing this, and replacing it will not change the water table.

## Signs a field may recover

- Symptoms began **after** an identifiable upstream event — a missed pumping interval, a baffle failure, a filter that was never cleaned
- The field is not uniformly wet; parts still look and behave normally
- Trouble tracks with weather or unusual household loading rather than being constant
- The system is relatively young and was properly sized when installed
- Ponding is localised over one lateral rather than across the whole field

## Signs it is genuinely finished

- Effluent surfacing across the **whole** field, not one area
- Constant saturation independent of weather or household use
- Symptoms unchanged after the tank is pumped, the baffles fixed and the box levelled
- Years of documented solids reaching the field
- Repeated failures at shortening intervals

## How the assessment should run

Take load off first — pump the tank so the field stops receiving flow. Open the distribution box and check it is level and clear. Assess the laterals for biomat, root intrusion and crushing. Establish whether the failure is loading-driven, mechanical or soil-driven. On karst ground, note any solution features near the field. Then check whether the parcel even has reserve area for a replacement, because that changes what your options are.

Only after all of that is a replacement recommendation worth anything.
